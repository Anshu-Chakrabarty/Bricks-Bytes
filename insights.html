LMS selection

An LMS shortlist is not a learning strategy.

Most LMS projects start in the wrong room. A team collects feature lists, sits through demonstrations, and tries to score platforms before anyone has agreed who the learners are, what they must be able to do, and which systems already hold the truth about people and products.

Selection is one move inside a larger piece of architecture. The useful questions are plain. Who learns — employees, customers, partners, distributors? What has to be configured: roles, groups, languages, locations? What content already exists, and what is still in someone's head? Which processes break if the platform cannot see HR, a product catalogue, or a service workflow?

A shortlist that ignores those questions will look decisive and age badly. Bricks & Bytes treats LMS selection, assessment and implementation planning as one conversation: the platform has to fit the people, the processes and the business objective, including the needs you do not have yet.

Learning architecture

What actually sits inside a learning ecosystem.

An ecosystem is not a login page with courses attached. It is the way technology, content, learners, administrators and learning processes work together. If any one of those is designed in isolation, the others have to compensate, usually by email.

Content is the part people can see, so it gets the attention. The quieter parts decide whether the content is used. User management decides who can reach it. Course deployment decides whether it is findable. Reporting decides whether anyone can tell participation from completion, and completion from usefulness. Administration is the daily work of keeping that honest.

That is why a rollout is more than a switch-on. Blueprint the strategy and the technology foundation. Build the content and configure the environment. Launch it to the people who need it. Then measure, refine and enhance. Skipping the last step is how a tidy launch becomes a stale catalogue.

Domain expertise

Domain training fails when it ignores the work.

A generic module can teach a policy. It cannot teach someone how a machine, a network, a habitat or a plant actually behaves. Sales teams for heavy equipment, technicians on an energy site, field staff installing telecom infrastructure, and rangers working around elephants are not the same learner with a different photograph dropped in.

The terminology, the risk and the moment of use are different. Product training for a distributor has to help them communicate value. Technical training has to make a complex system legible. Conservation learning has to support habitat safety, not a corporate tone of voice. Multi-brand equipment training has to give a service network one path through several manufacturers, or people will keep the real knowledge in binders.

Domain-specific training is slower to fake and faster to trust. It starts from the knowledge the industry already uses, then chooses the digital, instructor-led or blended route that fits how those people work.
